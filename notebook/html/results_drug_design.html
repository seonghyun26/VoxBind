1Vina evaluation and sample quality

1.1 Vina evaluation

Affinity and sample quality on the shared 79-pocket set, docked against the whole receptor at exhaustiveness 32 — the same protocol the published baselines use, so every row is directly comparable.

  • Set: the 79 CrossDocked test pockets that have usable ligand X-ray density. Docking against the whole receptor restores target_71, which the cropped protocol had to drop because pdb2pqr rejects its truncated pocket10 crop.
  • Protocol: ~100 molecules per pocket, Vina exhaustiveness 32, scored against the whole *_rec.pdb receptor. This matches the baseline protocol, so the prior-method rows and our rows are directly comparable. The cropped-receptor numbers this table replaces are kept in Table A5.
  • Calibration: the reference-ligand row is the control, and the row below quotes baseline.html so that every prior-method cell in this table comes from one source. Measured independently by our pipeline the same 79 crystal ligands land at −6.213 / −6.656 / −7.313 against that row's −6.21 / −6.66 / −7.32 — agreement within 0.007 kcal/mol on all three columns. Vina Score and Vina Min are deterministic and reproduce bit-identically across independent runs; only Vina Dock re-searches and therefore varies, which is the whole of the 0.007.
  • avg vs med — both are over molecules, on every row: the Vina avg and med columns, and the heavy-atom count, are the mean and the median of every generated molecule pooled across the 79 pockets (~7,900 values) — not of the 79 per-pocket values. This holds for the prior-method rows and for ours alike: the baselines are aggregated that way in baseline.html (appendix A), and our rows were re-aggregated to match. It is the published convention — DecompDiff states it collects all generated molecules across the test proteins and reports the mean and median over them, VoxBind states it computes the mean and median over all generated molecules, and targetdiff's evaluate_from_meta.py, FuncBind's aggregate_metrics.py and targetdiff's evaluate_diffusion.py all flatten to one per-molecule list in code. Neither column is the mean of the per-pocket medians, and neither is a per-pocket value: averaging within each pocket first moves rows by up to 0.25 kcal/mol — the per-pocket median of Vina Dock would read −8.30 and −8.78 for our two rows instead of −8.05 and −8.51. The reference-ligand row is the exception — one molecule per pocket, so both definitions coincide.
  • Still per-pocket: High aff. is the share of a pocket's molecules that out-dock that pocket's own reference, so it is per-pocket by construction and then averaged over the 79 (the baselines aggregate it the same way). Diversity is the mean pairwise Tanimoto within a pocket, so its mean and median are over the 79 pocket values — there is no per-molecule diversity to pool. Every other column, QED and SA included, is pooled per molecule on every row. eval_docking_results_full79.json keeps QED and SA only as a per-pocket mean, so for the three rows scored by our pipeline (recompute_qed_sa.py) they were re-derived per molecule from the stored SMILES; the recomputed per-pocket means reproduce the stored ones exactly, drift 0.0000.
  • Vina Score / Min / Dock: Score reads the pose as generated; Min relaxes it locally; Dock discards the pose and re-searches. Score and Min are outlier-dominated — a single clashing pose moves the average by more than a kcal/mol — so read the median column.
  • High aff.: fraction out-docking that pocket's own reference ligand. Diversity: 1 − mean pairwise Tanimoto within a pocket.

1.2 Ligand size vs performance

Heavy-atom count correlates with the per-pocket Vina Dock difference at ρ ≈ −0.8, so any raw Dock gain has to be read against molecule size before it can be called a binding gain.

Ours v1's gain survives the size control, two independent ways. Normalising each molecule's Vina Dock by its heavy-atom count (ligand efficiency) still favours v1 — −0.354 against vanilla's −0.344 kcal/mol per heavy atom, better on 47 of 78 pockets, p = 2.6×10−3. Binning by size (Figure 4) says the same thing: v1 is ahead in 7 of 9 size bins, so the −0.41 is not merely the effect of drawing larger molecules. The per-pocket paired statistics behind that −0.41 are in Table A1.

More atoms does not simply mean a better score — and that is where the two models separate. Below 30 heavy atoms the two curves sit on top of each other; the per-bin gaps of −0.2 to −0.35 in Table 2 are that small difference spread thin. What changes is the large end. Vanilla improves with size only to 33 atoms (−9.86) and then degrades — −9.24 at 36, −8.61 at 40 — while Ours v1 stays flat near −10. Aggregated over the bins the intervals do not overlap: vanilla goes −9.68 [−9.84, −9.51] at 32–35 to −9.16 [−9.31, −9.01] at 36+, where v1 goes −9.95 → −10.18. So v1 is less "better at large ligands" than not worse at them — and it draws more of them: 14.0% of its molecules carry 36+ heavy atoms against vanilla's 10.7%. The claim is not that bigger is better, but that density conditioning keeps the large end usable — the end that matters for filling a real pocket.

One caveat visible in Figure 4: at 44 heavy atoms the gap inverts (+1.29 in v1's favour becomes against it) on 26 and 42 molecules respectively, and the bootstrap band widens accordingly. It recovers to −1.48 at 45. Read the tail as a trend, not point by point.

Vanilla turns where its training data thins out — but that does not explain why Ours v1 does not. The training ligands' 90th percentile is 34 heavy atoms, so past ~34 both models are extrapolating beyond 90% of what they saw, and vanilla's minimum sits at 33. That is a clean coincidence and the obvious first explanation for the decline. It does not survive contact with the other row: Ours v1 pushes further out of distribution — 22.5% of its molecules carry 33+ heavy atoms against the training set's 14.3% — and holds −10 there while vanilla falls to −8.6. Same training data, same architecture; the density channel is the only difference. Whether the conditioning is what buys the out-of-distribution robustness is untested — the discriminating experiment is the apo variant, which has the same architecture with the ligand density masked out.
One recipe out of three works, and it is the one with a leakage caveat. Ours v1 conditions on the holo ligand X-ray density — the co-crystal map encodes where the real ligand sat, so part of the gain is informational rather than learned. The project's earlier apo variant, which masks the ligand density and removes that channel, dropped back to −7.92 Dock. Treat −8.24 as an upper bound on what density conditioning buys.

2PoseCheck and PoseBusters — structural quality of the generated poses

Strain energy and steric clashes from PoseCheck, plus the PoseBusters pass rate, on the same 78 pockets.

  • Strain energy: UFF internal energy of the generated conformer above its relaxed form — how contorted the molecule had to be to hold the pose. Reported as a median; the mean is dominated by a handful of poses where relaxation fails and is not usable.

  • PB-valid: share of molecules where every PoseBusters check passes. The report carries 22 check entries and valid is true only when all 22 are, though PoseBusters itself counts 20 of them in its own n_checks. Computed only over molecules whose PoseBusters worker completed — the subset size is given in the column, and it is not guaranteed unbiased.

  • The size control of Figure 4, asked of strain instead of Vina Dock: is a model's strain a property of the poses it makes, or of the molecules it happens to draw? Same 78 pockets, same n ≥ 20 and x ≤ 50 cut-offs, so the two figures line up.   • Size explains part of Ours's gap to vanilla, but not all of it. Pooled, v1 sits 30% above vanilla (82.3 against 63.2). Figure 8  ·  How fast strain grows with ligand size

  • Figure 7 shows where each method's strain sits; this one isolates how fast it grows. Top: the same per-size medians, each divided by that method's own fitted value at 6 heavy atoms, on a log axis — removing the vertical offset is what makes the slopes comparable by eye.   • Ordering by slope: Ours v1 +0.35 < vanilla +0.41 < reference +0.44 < Ours v2 +0.45 < TargetDiff +0.55 decades per 10 heavy atoms. The models that are worse on pooled strain also degrade faster with size, so their disadvantage widens on exactly the large ligands a real pocket needs.
  • Ours v1 is the shallowest of all five — shallower than the crystal references, which are single molecules per pocket and noisier. Its pooled strain is above vanilla's, but it is the one whose strain grows most slowly as ligands get bigger.

Figure 9  ·  PoseCheck strain energy at every ligand size — geometric mean

  • Why a second statistic. Figure 7 draws the median; this draws the geometric mean of the same values, because "does the mean say something different?" is a fair question of a skewed distribution. The arithmetic mean cannot be used: 4–7% of molecules per model fail UFF relaxation and land at 104–1010 kcal/mol, dragging the pooled arithmetic mean to 2.3×108 for vanilla and 4.3×109 for Ours v1 — numbers that report the failure rate, not the strain. The geometric mean is the arithmetic mean of log-strain, the natural centre for a positive heavy-tailed quantity already on a log axis, and unlike the median it still moves when the whole upper half of the distribution shifts.
  • The mean does not rescue the pooled comparison. Ours v1 stays behind vanilla on every statistic: median 82.3 vs 63.2, geometric mean 130.4 vs 87.5, 5%-trimmed arithmetic mean 5,549 vs 2,485. The cumulative view of Figure 5 is not an artefact of choosing the median.
  • What changes is the large end — and it changes by more. The curves cross near 33 heavy atoms and Ours v1 stays below vanilla out to 50. Over the 36+ bin the ordering flips on all three statistics, and the mean-family gaps are the wider ones: median 372.7 → 222.3 (1.7×), geometric mean 607 → 375 (1.6×), 5%-trimmed mean 4,481 → 1,024 (4.4×). Read with Figure 4: 33 atoms is also where vanilla's Vina Dock stops improving, so the same size region is where vanilla loses both binding and pose quality.
  • Caveat. Strain can be ≤ 0 when the 50-conformer global relaxation fails to beat the locally-relaxed pose; those cannot be logged and are floored at 10−9, which pulls the geometric mean down slightly — the conservative direction for whichever model has more of them. Bands are 95% bootstrap CIs (1,500 resamples, seed 260827); curves stop where fewer than 20 molecules share an atom count.

Vina Dock is negative-is-better, so a negative ρ means more strain goes with stronger docking and a positive ρ means it goes with weaker docking. ρ | heavy atoms is the partial Spearman with molecule size removed. Molecules are joined to their docking score by SMILES, keeping only unambiguous one-to-one matches, which is why n is below the molecule counts in Table 4.

Two-thirds of every PoseBusters failure is one check: flat saturated rings. non-aromatic_ring_non-flatness fails on 18.4% of vanilla's molecules and 16.1% of Ours v1's, and for most of those it is the only failing check (16.9% and 14.3%). Exclude it and both models jump to about 90% valid — and the ordering inverts: vanilla 91.1% against v1's 90.0%. So v1's PB-valid edge in Table 4 (75.6% vs 74.2%) is not evidence of better-placed poses; it is entirely that v1 draws fewer flat saturated rings. The crystal references never fail this check at all, which makes it a generative artefact rather than a property of real ligands.
On clashes, moving from vanilla to v1 moves toward the crystal ligands, not away. Table 4 reads clashes as lower-is-better and puts vanilla first, but the reference row is 6.82 and the caption already warns that zero is not the target. Normalised for size the ordering is vanilla 2.18 clashes per 10 heavy atoms, Ours v1 2.60, crystal ligands 2.99, Ours v2 3.34, TargetDiff 4.65. Vanilla is not landing closest to the crystal target — it is the furthest below it, and v1 sits between the two. TargetDiff overshoots in the other direction.
Ours v1 makes measurably more protein–ligand contact than vanilla — but the gain is mostly a proximity count that overlaps with the clash column. Paired over the 78 pockets and normalised per 10 heavy atoms, v1 gains +0.36 interactions (p = 1.6×10−6). About 70% of that is VdWContact (+0.26, p = 1.4×10−6), which correlates with the steric-clash count at ρ ≈ 0.65 (0.61 with size partialled out). So “more contact” and “more clashes” are substantially the same observation rather than two independent ones, and the interaction profile corroborates the tighter-packing reading without being fully independent evidence for it. Hydrogen bonds contribute the rest of the gain (+0.10, p = 2.2×10−3); hydrophobic contact does not move at all (+0.003, p = 0.74).
Both models have the same wrongly-shaped contact profile, and density conditioning barely changes its shape. Against the crystal ligands in the same pockets, per 10 heavy atoms, both models make about 1.5× too much hydrophobic contact (v1 and vanilla are identical here, 0.88 against the reference's 0.60) and about half the hydrogen bonds (v1 0.84, vanilla 0.74, reference 1.47). v1 reaches crystal-level van der Waals density — 0.95×, statistically indistinguishable (p = 0.42), where vanilla sits significantly below at 0.89× (p = 2.3×10−3) — and it closes roughly a seventh of the hydrogen-bond gap (0.50 → 0.57 of the crystal rate). Shape complementarity is what the density channel buys. Polar complementarity is where every model on this page is furthest from a real ligand, and it stays that way.
Strain does not buy affinity — that appearance is the size confound again. Pooled, strain correlates negatively with Vina Dock for every model (ρ = −0.29 to −0.58), which reads as “more strained poses dock harder”. Partial out heavy-atom count and every one of those correlations flips sign (ρ = +0.08 to +0.21), and it stays positive in every four-atom size bin above 8 heavy atoms (the 4–7 bin is the only exception, at ρ ≈ 0). Big molecules simply strain more and dock better; at matched size a strained pose docks worse. The same control leaves clashes essentially unrelated to docking (ρ = −0.14 to +0.15). This is the third place on this page where controlling for ligand size changes the answer.

2.3 Calibration and caveats

What these numbers can and cannot be compared against.

Calibration, and one thing it does not cover. Our measured TargetDiff clash mean reproduces the ~10.8 reported in the VoxBind paper, so the gaps above are a property of the models rather than of our scoring. Strain is a different story: PoseCheck changed its strain definition after that paper was published and we run the current pinned release (1.3.1), so the strain column is internally consistent across these rows but is not comparable to any published strain figure. Compare strain within this page only.
PoseBusters ties the two models PoseCheck separates. Ours v1's PB-valid rate (75.6%) is marginally above vanilla's (74.2%) even though it carries 1.3× the strain. PoseBusters asks pass-or-fail against a threshold; PoseCheck measures how much. The reading is that v1 is not producing broken geometry — it is producing poses that sit tighter against the pocket wall, which is what density conditioning trains it to do. This is a hypothesis, not a tested claim.

Our TargetDiff run scores below the published TargetDiff row — and most of the gap is accounted for. Vina Dock −7.49 against the paper's −7.80, a gap of 0.31 kcal/mol. Two of the three candidate causes have been measured rather than left open:
  • Pocket selection is ruled out. Table A2 moves Vina Dock by 0.08 across the 100/92/78 sets and every quality column by less than 0.01, so which pockets we score on cannot carry a 0.31 gap. Every row on this page also shares one pocket set, so the axis is neutral for within-table comparisons.

Within-table comparisons share the protocol exactly and are unaffected by any of this; only the comparison against the paper row carries the offset.
